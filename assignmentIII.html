Project III: Final Presentation

Audience

For this project, I focused on targeting an audience interested in social justice and humanitarian issues, particularly those who may be unfamiliar with the food scarcity crisis in Cuba. This issue is not widely discussed, so I aimed to make the story accessible and informative, including a timeline that covers key moments in Cuba’s economic and sociopolitical history to provide context for the data and narrative.

I geared the writing toward a college-aged audience. However, I also wanted this to be as accessible as possible. To ensure clarity, I broke the information down step-by-step, accompanying each data point with a description of the chart and an explanation of what the data represents.

Action Items

From the previous phase of the project found here, I developed a list of action items based on the feedback I received:

  • Refine the Story Flow: One challenge I faced was determining the order of information in a story where multiple factors influence this issue. To streamline the narrative, I narrowed the focus to only sociopolitical and economic factors, excluding political and environmental elements. As part of my call to action, I encourage readers to educate themselves further (and hopefully uncover the rest of the story).
  • Finalize Graphs: I accomplished this by narrowing the scope of the data! By focusing on just the sociopolitical and economic aspects, it became easier to select the most relevant data for the story.
  • Clarify Personal Experience: To address previous feedback, I limited my personal connection to the introduction and avoided integrating it into the timeline to prevent confusion.
  • Anecdote Conclusion: While I didn't end with an anecdote, I concluded the story by discussing the Cuban concept of "resolver" as a way to draw attention back to the Cuban people directly impacted.

Design Decisions

Preparation: To organize my project, I laid out the text in a table, dividing it by section to help track each part more effectively. One of the most challenging aspects was organizing the sources and keeping track of where to link them. Fortunately, Shorthand was compatible with text that already had hyperlinks, which made the process easier.

Data Visualizations: I used far less data than I initially collected, as I trimmed some of the story content to keep the focus tight. This made connecting the data to the text and ensuring a smooth story flow much simpler. I chose bar graphs, line charts, and a doughnut chart. Each visualization was attributed to its data source, displayed both within and beneath the visualization.

Color: For the visualizations, I kept the color scheme simple. For example, in the line graph showing survey data on Cubans skipping meals, I used red for those who skip meals and green for those who don’t. The overall color palette for the site was neutral—earthy tones like olive green, off-white, and cream—to avoid distracting from the story’s serious subject matter.

Multimodality: As a visual learner with a background in education, I wanted to offer audiences multiple ways to engage with the story. Shorthand’s interactive features allowed viewers to explore the text dynamically, which I found engaging. To encourage further exploration, I included hyperlinks to additional resources. I also incorporated a video featuring street interviews with Cubans discussing the crisis, as I felt hearing directly from those affected was powerful.

Additional Visuals: While most images came from Unsplash, I also contributed some of my own, either by using family photographs or creating simple graphics. I often found it easier to create visual elements, like the timeline or headers, as standalone images rather than using Shorthand's formatting options. This allowed for greater control over design choices, including fonts and layout.

Reflection

At the beginning of each phase of this project, I was concerned about whether I would feel satisfied with the final outcome. Since this is a topic I’m deeply invested in, I didn’t want to misrepresent the situation or create something that felt unappealing or inaccessible. Initially, I found it intimidating to integrate data into the story, especially since I have more experience with visual communication.

In the end, I’m pleased with the results. This experience has taught me the value of building wireframes, gathering feedback, and developing the project in stages. I’ve already begun applying this process to a personal project and plan to use it for future projects as well!
